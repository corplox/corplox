What Mexico and the U.S. Actually Traded in 2025

Two neighbors, almost $800 billion in goods trade — and a surprisingly different product mix in each direction.

2026 edition · 2025 data · Source: UN Comtrade

Mexico and the United States are often discussed through tariffs, trade deficits and manufacturing jobs. Those debates matter, but the product-level figures are interesting on their own. They show what actually moved across the border, at least as recorded in customs statistics.

In 2025, the recorded two-way merchandise trade value was about $795.31 billion. Mexico recorded $545.39 billion in exports to the United States and $249.92 billion in imports from the United States.

The two directions do not look alike

Once the figures are grouped by product sector, the difference is clear. The biggest flows from Mexico to the U.S. were machinery, vehicles and electrical machinery. In the other direction, machinery and electronics were important too, but fuels and plastics stood out much more.

What Mexico shipped to the United States

Three sectors dominated Mexico's recorded exports to its northern neighbor: machinery and mechanical appliances ($146.85B), vehicles and parts ($127.57B) and electrical machinery and electronics ($98.89B). Together those flows were worth over $373 billion.

That is a much more manufacturing-heavy picture than one might expect from discussions focused mainly on agricultural exports or raw materials. Optical, measuring and medical instruments also accounted for nearly $29.98B.

And what moved back toward Mexico?

In the opposite direction, Mexico recorded $35.46B in machinery imports from the U.S., $34.51B in mineral fuels and oils, $26.97B in electrical machinery and $24.37B in vehicles and parts.

The fuel comparison is hard to miss: the U.S.-to-Mexico flow was about $34.51B, versus $4.73B from Mexico to the U.S. Plastics showed a smaller reversal, at $18.69B from the U.S. to Mexico compared with $10.09B the other way.

Meanwhile, categories including furniture, beverages, fruit and vegetables were much larger in the Mexico-to-U.S. direction.

What these numbers cannot tell us

A customs record describes merchandise moving between reporting partners. It does not, by itself, say who owns a factory, where profits are paid, or how much of a product's value was added in a particular country. A vehicle exported from Mexico may contain components from several places, and a factory in Mexico may have foreign or domestic owners.

There is also a reporting detail: the Mexico-to-U.S. series here represents exports reported by Mexico. The U.S.-to-Mexico series represents imports reported by Mexico from the United States. Those aren't necessarily identical to the numbers that would appear in a report using the United States as the reporting country.

This is merchandise trade only. Services are not included.

Why the comparison matters

A single trade balance can hide how different the two product mixes are. Looking at the sectors side by side helps make the trade relationship more concrete — machinery and vehicles traveling north, but significant flows of fuel, plastics and machinery traveling south.

It is a starting point for understanding the relationship, not a complete measure of who gains or loses from it.
